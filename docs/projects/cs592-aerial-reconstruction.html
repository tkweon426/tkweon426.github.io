Motion Blur in Aerial 3D Reconstruction

CS592: Autonomous Drones · Purdue University

My motion blur analysis for a team study of aerial 3D reconstruction and semantic understanding, including FFT-based frame filtering and 3D Gaussian Splatting evaluation.

Team: Yun Seok Gwon and Hyunchang Kweon (Thomas)

Can removing blurred drone images improve a 3D reconstruction, even when it leaves fewer training views? My main contribution to this project was the motion blur analysis, examining FFT-based image scoring, frame filtering, and their effect on 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS). I also helped explore the project’s broader questions about flight altitude, reconstruction quality, and semantic understanding.

At the best tested threshold on one UAVScenes sequence, filtering improved held-out PSNR from 20.452 to 21.568 dB, a gain of approximately 1.12 dB.

My contribution and project context

The team investigated two challenges in creating useful 3D representations from drone imagery: the effect of flight altitude on geometry and semantics, and the effect of motion blur on reconstruction fidelity.

My main contribution focused on the second question: how to quantify blur without a sharp reference image, how aggressively to filter the training images, and how the resulting reconstructions compare on held-out views. I also contributed to exploration of the other areas, including 3DGS, CLIP, SAM 2, and language-embedded scene representations.

The report’s Part II, Sections VII–X (pages 7–9) covers the motion blur study. The altitude and semantic experiments are presented as shared project context below.

Quantifying motion blur with FFT

Motion blur suppresses fine image detail, reducing high-frequency content. The pipeline uses this observation to assign each frame a reference-free sharpness score:

  1. Convert the RGB image to grayscale.
  2. Apply a two-dimensional Hann window to reduce boundary artifacts.
  3. Compute a 2D Fast Fourier Transform and its power spectrum.
  4. Split the spectrum into low-, mid-, and high-frequency radial bands.
  5. Combine the mid- and high-frequency energy ratios into a weighted score.

The score uses weights of 0.6 for high-frequency energy and 0.4 for mid-frequency energy, scaled by 1,000. Higher scores indicate sharper images. Filtering keeps frames whose scores meet or exceed the selected threshold.

Source: Figure 8, report, page 7.

Isolating the effect on reconstruction

The experiment used a 336-frame subset of the UAVScenes HKAirport_GNSS_Evening sequence. Ten manually selected sharp frames were held out for testing and excluded from every training subset. Rejection thresholds ranged from 0.0 to 2.0, in steps of 0.2, producing progressively sharper subsets of the remaining images.

To reduce the influence of camera-registration failures, the study bypassed Structure-from-Motion: it used dataset-provided camera poses and LiDAR points for Gaussian initialization. This setup was intended to isolate the effect of blurred training images on 3DGS optimization.

Source: Figure 9, report, page 8.

The reconstructions were evaluated on the same held-out images using PSNR and SSIM, where higher is better, and LPIPS, where lower is better.

Results

Source: Figure 10, report, page 9.

Metric No rejection (0.0) Best tested threshold (2.0) Change
PSNR (dB, higher is better) 20.452 21.568 +1.116
SSIM (higher is better) 0.632 0.674 +0.042
LPIPS (lower is better) 0.556 0.477 −0.079

Values from Table VII, report, page 8; changes calculated from the reported baseline and threshold-2.0 values.

Small threshold increases produced modest, sometimes non-monotonic changes. The clearest gains appeared beyond approximately 1.2, with the strictest tested setting performing best on all three metrics. In this experiment, removing additional blurred views improved reconstruction quality despite reducing the number of training images.

One explanation discussed in the report is that moderately blurred frames retain displaced edges that conflict with sharper views during optimization. This is a hypothesis consistent with the observed trends; the threshold sweep does not establish that mechanism on its own.

What the results establish

The experiment supports blur-aware data selection for this sequence, with several limits:

  • One sequence and ten test views: broader validation is needed across scenes, trajectories, and lighting conditions.
  • Scene-dependent scores: texture changes the frequency distribution, so a threshold of 2.0 is not a universal setting.
  • Unmeasured sparsity limit: performance had not begun to decline at the highest tested threshold; the experiment did not locate the point where too few views hurt reconstruction.
  • Dataset-provided initialization: the evaluation does not measure the complete effect of filtering on an SfM-based pipeline.

Shared exploration: altitude and semantics

The wider project evaluated aerial reconstruction alongside CLIP-based semantic recognition and SAM 2 segmentation. Across the evaluated sequences, lower-altitude imagery often supported stronger geometric reconstruction, while higher-altitude imagery provided more object-level context for semantic recognition. Some higher-altitude subsets also suffered SfM failures.

Source: Figure 4, report, page 5.

The team also explored how annotation granularity affects SAM 2 boundary scores. Fine predicted boundaries can disagree with coarser human polygons, complicating interpretation of the metric.

These experiments examined components of a potential language-queryable aerial map. The reported CLIP evaluation used ground-truth region crops; full evaluation of SAM-generated crops and altitude-stratified LangSplatV2 language fields was deferred. The project therefore motivates further work on integrated systems without demonstrating a complete end-to-end solution.
